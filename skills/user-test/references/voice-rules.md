# Voice Rules

Each persona section is written in THAT PERSONA'S voice. A 58-year-old skeptic does not sound like a 24-year-old skimmer.

---

## Rules

- No UX jargon. No "affordances," "discoverability," "onboarding flow." Real language only.
- Short sentences when frustrated. Longer when impressed.
- Direct quotes of what they were thinking. Not paraphrases.
- The score must match the words. A 5/10 should sound like a 5/10.
- Do not soften findings with "however" or "to be fair" unless genuinely warranted.

---

## Weak vs. Strong

| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| "Navigation could be clearer" | "I clicked that three times and it took me to the same wrong page." |
| "Loading was slow" | "I stared at a blank area for 4 seconds and refreshed because I thought it broke." |
| "The design is clean" | "It looks like a real company made this. The colors feel trustworthy." |
| "Information hierarchy needs improvement" | "The page had 6 paragraphs. I read the bold one near the top. The rest I scrolled past." |
| "The app shows promise" | "I closed the tab. Not because it was broken — I couldn't figure out what to do." |

---

## The Honesty Rule

These testers are not trying to be helpful to the developer. They are trying to accomplish a goal. They don't care about technical constraints. They don't give credit for effort. They just want the thing to work, and if it doesn't, they leave.

A tester who says "this was fine" when it wasn't is useless. A tester who says "this was broken and I nearly left" when that's true is invaluable. Write the latter.

The most useful thing this skill produces is the thing the developer didn't know was a problem. Not the obvious bugs — those get found by QA. The invisible ones: the sentence nobody reads, the button nobody clicks, the page nobody finds, the color that makes the app look untrustworthy, the moment trust drops because something violated an expectation from a competitor. Those are what user testing is for.
